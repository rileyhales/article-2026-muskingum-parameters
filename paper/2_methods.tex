\subsection{Analysis of Muskingum Parameters}
\label{subsec:analysis-muskingum-parameters}

The Muskingum equation is given in Equation~\ref{eq:muskingum} \citep{usace_hms}.
$Q$ and $I$ are channel flows in cubic meters per second at the indicated time step and river reach.
The three coefficients, $c_1$, $c_2$, and $c_3$ are provided in are given in Equations~\ref{eq:c1},~\ref{eq:c2}, and~\ref{eq:c3}.
The coefficients are functions of the $k$ and $x$ parameters and the computation time step $\Delta t$.
Muskingum is stable, meaning that errors are not generated or amplified by the equation, for any $x$ between 0.0 and 0.5 \citep{mccarthy1938}.
Valid values of $k$ and $\Delta t$ are positive numbers.

\begin{equation}
  Q_{t+1} = c_1\, I_{t+1} + c_2\, I_t + c_3\, Q_t
  \label{eq:muskingum}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
  c_1 = \frac{\Delta t / k - 2x}{\Delta t / k + 2 (1-x)}
  \label{eq:c1}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
  c_2 = \frac{\Delta t / k + 2x}{\Delta t / k + 2 (1-x)}
  \label{eq:c2}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
  c_3 = \frac{2 (1-x) - \Delta t / k}{\Delta t / k + 2 (1-x)}
  \label{eq:c3}
\end{equation}

Though the equation is numerically stable, invalid combinations of $k$, $x$, and $\Delta t$ can produces physically valid results.
The source of the errors come from having negative coefficients \citep{hjelmfelt1985, chow1988, gill1992}.
Valid ranges of coefficients are described by the inequalities in Equation~\ref{eq:validity-conditions}.
The three coefficients share a common denominator which is positive for all valid values of $k$, $x$, and $\Delta t$.
The numerator of $c_2$ is always positive reducing the validity condition to values which produce positive numerators of $c_1$ and $c_3$.
When $x$is at the maximum of 0.5, meaning no attenuation, $\Delta t$ and $k$ must be set equal.
When $x$ is at the minimum of 0.0, meaning maximum attenuation, $\Delta t$ must be less than $2k$.

\begin{gather}
  c1: \Delta t >= 2 k x \notag \\
  c3: \Delta t <= 2 k (1-x) \notag \\
  2 k x <= \Delta t <= 2 k (1-x)
  \label{eq:validity-conditions}
\end{gather}

\subsection{Mitigation Strategies for Invalid Parameterizations}
\label{subsec:mitigation-strategies}

The first case of negative coefficients is when $c_1$ is negative meaning that $k > \Delta t$.
In terms of the river segment, the flood wave travel time and analagous reach length is too large relative to the time step.
The inflow at the next time step $I_{t+1}$ is subtracted from the outflow $Q_{t+1}$ in this case which is physically incorrect.
Larger inflows should produce larger outflows after some travel time lag and attenuation according to Muskingum's wedge and prism storage model.
This phenomenon is documented in. % todo: add citations.

One solution is to reduce the length of the river.
The river could be edited in the source GIS data by splitting the segment and catchment area into two or more subunits creating more segments with shorter lengths.
Alternatively, the same result can be achieved by dividing the river synthetically during the computation process.
That process is an adaptation of the Muskingum Cunge process where river discharge is calculated at incremental $\Delta x$ distances along the reach. % todo add citation
Rivers that are on the long end of the length distribution should be analyzed to for this condition.

The second condition of negative coefficients is when $c_3$ is negative meaning that $k < \Delta t$.
When $c_3$ is negative, the outflow at the previous time step $Q_t$ is subtracted from the outflow at the next time step $Q_{t+1}$.
A negative $c_3$ can produce negative discharge on $Q_{t+1}$ if $Q_t$ is larger than the inflow terms $I_{t+1}$ and $I_t$.
Subsequent time steps then weight the negative $Q_{t}$ with negative $c_3$ switching back to a positive $Q_{t+1}$.
The oscillation dampens over time because the solution is numerically stable. % todo: add citations.

A solution to this problem is to decrease the computational time step $\Delta t$.
Modelers may hesitate to decrease $\Delta t$ because code implementations only allow a single time step for all rivers.
The number of compute cycles increases inversely proportional with $\Delta t$ such that decreasing $\Delta t$ to $1 / n$ of its original multiplies the total compute by a factor of $n$.
Cutting the time step by half causes twice the number of compute cycles on every river, decreasing to a quarter means four times the number of compute cycles, and so on.
Rivers on the shortest end of the length distribution should be analyzed for this condition.
Addiitionally, the code implementation of Muskingum may need to allow for decreased $\Delta t$ at some rivers sufficiently address this condition without multiplying the compute costs.

\subsection{Analysis of Global Hydrography Products}
\label{subsec:analysis-hydrography-products}

We surveyed a total of six global hydrography products assessing their fitness for routing calculations. The datasets are HydroRIVERS (HydroSHEDS v1)
\citep{lehner2013},HydroSHEDS~v2 \citep{lehner2022, lehner2026}, MERIT-Basins \citep{lin2019}, NHDPlus~V2 \citep{mckay2012}, TDX-Hydro
\citep{carlson2024}, and GRIT v1 \citep{wortmann_grit, wortmann2025}. Four of these products have global coverage. The three most recently produced
datasets, HydroSHEDS v2, TDX-Hydro, and GRIT, are derived from the 12-meter TanDEM-X elevations or the FABDEM 30-meter elevations derived from
TanDEM-X. Two, HydroRIVERS and MERIT-Basins, ultimately derive from the 90-meter SRTM elevations. NHDPlus~v2 comes from USGS 3DEP elevation data which
is the only non-global elevation product represented.

To assess the distribution of stream reaches, we calculate $k = L / c$ assuming a flood wave celerity of 1~m/s.
The wave celerity assumption is for exploratory purposes labeling streams in units applicable to the validity conditions in Equation~\ref{eq:validity-conditions}.
Each dataset provided a length attribute, either in meters or kilometers.
There were 23,585 lines of NHDPlus~V2, which trace the shoreline rather than a channel which we ignored.
GRIT traces bifurcations in rivers but we only analyzed those marked as the primary channel

Streams were analyzed primarily for the distribution of their reach lengths and their $k$ parameters, calculated as assuming
constant 1~m/s velocity. Using a fixed velocity for all streams keeps the distribution of $k$ identical to the distribution of lengths and functions
as a unit converter.

We apply a Courant condition to the analysis assisting in the assessment of interpretting the validity conditions in Equation~\ref{eq:validity-conditions}.

\subsection{Analysis of Existing Routing Code Implementations and Test Watersheds}
\label{subsec:analysis-of-routing-code}

This article analyzes Muskingum implementations of WRF-Hydro as extracted in t-route \citep{troute_code} and RAPID \citep{david2011}.
These two are the used on several instances of large domain models fitting the profile described in Section~\ref{sec:introduction}.
We acknowledge several other implementations of interest but are not as commonly used for the specific cases being explored in this article.
One example is LISFLOOD \citep{vanderknijff2010} which uses kinematic wave routing on grids rather than vectors and is used for the European Flood Awareness System \citep{smith2016,matthews2025} and the Global Flood Awareness System \citep{alfieri2013, harrigan2020, prudhomme2024}.

The purpose of the code review is to establish how these implementations handle negative coefficient cases.
Some specific cases being looked for include determining if invalid cases are checked for, accepted by the algorithm, and if warnings at runtime or in documentation are given.
Both implementations are written in Fortran.
The authors are the lead developers or well experienced in both code bases.
As an additional layer of analysis, we also applied static checking tools and AI code review tools to verify the findings.
